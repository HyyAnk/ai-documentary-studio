### Research Standard

- Prefer a clear timeline, decision, mechanism, or cause-and-effect chain.
- Prefer claims that can be supported by primary sources, archives, records, or reputable reporting.
- Separate confirmed facts, reasonable inference, and unresolved uncertainty.
- Reject a topic when its central claim depends on speculation, outrage, or a generic listicle premise.
- Record the source trail for dates, numbers, quotes, ownership, and causal claims.
- Label claims internally as confirmed, inferred, disputed, or unknown.

## Episode Structure

1. **Cold open:** show the central contradiction, mystery, or visual question.
2. **Expectation:** establish what people believed, wanted, designed, or attempted.
3. **Pressure:** explain the system, decision, incentive, or constraint that shaped events.
4. **Turning point:** identify the moment when the expected outcome became unlikely.
5. **Consequence:** show what changed and who or what was affected.
6. **Legacy:** explain what remains, what replaced it, or what the story teaches.

## Narrative Style

- Write for the ear: concrete verbs, short sentences at turning points, and no filler introduction.
- Make the viewer understand why the decision seemed reasonable at the time before judging the result.
- Prefer one strong through-line over a collection of loosely related facts.
- Keep the ending specific: answer the core question without flattening the nuance.

## Visual Language

### Preferred Visual Modes

- archival material and document textures;
- historical or conceptual reconstruction;
- maps, timelines, diagrams, and before/after comparisons;
- product, architecture, interface, or environment reconstruction;
- macro details and scale transitions;
- restrained motion graphics that clarify the narration.

### Continuity Rules

- Keep period, geography, materials, lighting, camera language, and subject identity consistent.
- Give each scene one primary visual idea.
- Dialogue and visual prompt must describe the same moment.
- Use stylization to signal reconstruction; never present invented imagery as authentic evidence.

## AI and Fact Rules

- Never invent a quote, source, person, statistic, event, or archival image as fact.
- Mark reconstructed scenes internally and preserve a source trail for decisive claims.
- Use AI to explain, visualize, or reconstruct—not to replace evidence.
- If a detail is uncertain, state the uncertainty in the research notes and avoid false precision.
